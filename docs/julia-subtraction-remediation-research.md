# Subtraction remediation: research and proposed curriculum

Research date: 15 September 2026. This is the pre-implementation research snapshot.
The subsequent implementation is documented in [Subtraction Steps](./subtraction-steps.md).

## Recommendation

Build the focused trainer. The combination of prerequisite subtraction practice, brief fluency work, targeted repetition, and progressively harder written calculations is reasonable. Keep the five requested tiers, vertical presentation, handwriting-only answers, and a writable question area.

Treat the 3/4/5/6/7-second targets and eight-answer advancement rule as **product choices to evaluate**, not scientifically established mastery thresholds or official Kumon standards. The main design improvements are representative promotion checks, spaced review, accurate handwriting timing, and a separate measure of transfer to the actual assignment.

### Confirmed brief

- Current assignment reported by the parent: approximately B160, three-digit minus two-digit subtraction, roughly 90 questions in 10 minutes.
- Tier targets: use the individual 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7-second targets, rather than requiring three seconds at every tier.
- Answer entry is exclusively drawn. Present every exercise vertically.
- Let the learner write anywhere in the question area, including borrow marks.
- Start with simpler subtraction; move forward when the learner demonstrates the requested speed and consistency.
- Favor slow or inaccurate facts at lower tiers and weak question features at higher tiers.

The learner's age, current accuracy, device, and actual problem distribution were not supplied. Nothing below infers a diagnosis or an age-based standard. The reported assignment content and instructor target are the practical reference, rather than assuming that a worksheet number has an identical meaning across editions and countries.

## 1. What the evidence supports

These sources support the ingredients of the approach. None evaluates this exact five-tier, stylus-input, eight-answer system.

| Source | Finding relevant to this trainer | Limits on the conclusion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [IES/What Works Clearinghouse, 2021, Recommendation 6](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/26) | Rates timed fluency activities as supported by strong evidence. The full guide recommends brief activities, generally 1–5 minutes, on previously learned material, with efficient strategies, feedback, and error correction. | Its evidence includes instructional packages, not a controlled test of a particular per-question deadline. It does not establish the proposed gates. |
| [Fuchs et al., 2013, randomized first-grade tutoring study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24065865/) | Among 591 at-risk students assigned to three conditions, number-knowledge tutoring with speeded practice produced stronger arithmetic and two-digit calculation outcomes than tutoring with nonspeeded practice. Tutoring lasted 16 weeks; practice occupied 5 minutes of each 30-minute lesson. | Speed practice accompanied substantial instruction. This does not show that unsupported drills alone produce the same result, or predict an individual child's improvement. |
| [McNeil, Jordan, Viegut & Ansari, 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/15291006241287726) | This research synthesis recommends explicit strategies, structured retrieval practice, and time-limited work after accuracy develops, with opportunities to reflect on number relationships. | It concerns arithmetic fluency broadly. Automatic basic facts and full written multi-column procedures are different performance targets. |
| [Schutte et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25746824/) | Forty-eight third graders practiced addition facts for four minutes daily across 19 days. Spreading practice across the day produced greater fluency growth than doing all four minutes consecutively. | Small study of addition facts. It does not prescribe the optimal spacing for this learner or three-digit subtraction. |
| [Mettler, Massey & Kellman, 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6028005/) | Two experiments found that scheduling practice using response time and accuracy outperformed fixed schedules on immediate and delayed retention. | Supports adaptive scheduling as a principle; does not validate this subtraction feature model or any proposed weighting percentages. |
| [Koponen et al., 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01187/full) | A strategy-training study used correct responses within three seconds in an individual addition-fact task. The authors explicitly recognized that fast derived strategies can also meet that limit. | Three seconds is an operational research criterion in a particular task, not a universal handwriting cutoff or proof of memorized retrieval. |
| [Ebersbach & Barzagar Nazari, 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00811/full) | An experiment with 235 university students found no robust spacing benefit for a mathematical procedure under the tested conditions. | Different population and task, but a useful boundary: evidence about fact retention should not be treated as a guarantee for every multi-step calculation. |

**Design inference:** use short, supported practice to improve an already understood process. If mistakes cluster around regrouping, briefly teach that step; if answers are correct but slow, emphasize retrieval and efficient execution. A response time alone cannot establish which of these is the bottleneck.

## 2. Alignment with Kumon

### The philosophy broadly matches

Kumon's [individualized lesson-planning guidance](https://www.kumon.com/resources/canadian_english/how-does-kumons-individualized-lesson-planning-work/) describes repetition, accuracy, completion time, and instructor observation as inputs to progression. Its [current completion-time guidance](https://www.kumon.com/resources/understanding-completion-time-in-kumon-a-parents-practical-guide/) emphasizes that expected times vary across worksheet sets and that unusually slow work can have several causes. This supports an adaptive supplement, but not a claim that eight fast answers automatically establish Kumon readiness.

### Published timing figures need their units and edition

The [math table linked from Kumon Singapore's downloads page](https://kumon.sg/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Table-of-Learning-Maths.pdf) is dated **October 2016**, although its upload path is newer. On page 2 it defines SCT in minutes per sheet, including corrections. It lists B121–150 two-digit subtraction at 2–3 minutes per sheet, B151–160 mixed two-digit work at 2–4, and B161–170 three-digit subtraction at 2–3.

This regional, older chart does not exactly match the reported B160 content. It is useful context, not grounds to override the parent's description or declare the current instructor's target wrong. A worksheet-side sample is not enough to infer the question count per complete sheet or assignment.

### The assignment gives the most useful numerical benchmark

Using the parent's approximate count:

**600 seconds / 90 questions = 6.67 seconds per question = 9 questions per minute.**

| Average time per question | Time for 90 questions, before extra overhead |
| --- | --- |
| 7.0 seconds | 10 minutes 30 seconds |
| 6.67 seconds | Approximately 10 minutes |
| 6.0 seconds | 9 minutes |

The tier-5 rule that each of eight answers is under seven seconds is a reasonable intermediate gate. It does **not imply** a whole-assignment average of 6.67 seconds, particularly when corrections and transitions consume time. Conversely, some answers can exceed 6.67 seconds while a worksheet still meets its overall target.

Recommendation: retain the requested seven-second tier gate and add a separate, parent-facing readiness measure. A roughly six-second average on representative, fresh tier-5 problems would provide about one minute of headroom across 90 problems. That six-second figure is arithmetic-based design judgment, not a published standard. Verify transfer on the real assignment under the instructor's timing and correction rules.

### Borrow marks are a deliberate difference

[Kumon's official UK guidance](https://www.kumon.co.uk/blog/doing-calculations-without-writing-carries-and-borrows) encourages calculation without written carry/borrow marks, while acknowledging that writing them is mathematically acceptable. This establishes Kumon's stated instructional preference; it is not independent evidence for every broader benefit claimed in the article.

Keep the requested writable surface. Make marks optional and never count them as an error or silently disqualify an otherwise valid tier-gate answer. Parent reporting can distinguish attempts with and without scratch ink. If the instructor expects no written regrouping, readiness must eventually be checked under that condition. Scratch ink indicates use of the workspace, not proof of a particular mental strategy.

## 3. Proposed five-tier curriculum

These are recommended boundaries and teaching sequences, not implemented changes.

| Tier | Requested content and time | Suggested internal sequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Single digit minus single digit; at most 3 seconds | Nonnegative facts using digits 0–9. Include zero and equal-number facts but cap their frequency so they cannot dominate promotion. Mix subtracting small amounts with close-number differences. |
| 2 | Under-20 subtraction; at most 4 seconds | Prioritize teen-minus-single-digit facts, particularly crossing ten, while retaining earlier facts. Teach useful relationships such as making ten and using known differences. |
| 3 | Under-20 review plus two digits minus one digit; under 5 seconds | No regrouping, regrouping from tens, multiples of ten, then mixed practice. Keep under-20 facts as review while ensuring the gate actually samples the newly added content. |
| 4 | Two digits minus two digits; under 6 seconds | No regrouping, regrouping into ones, zero in ones, and one-digit results. Vary the tens digits so learning generalizes beyond exact examples. |
| 5 | Three digits minus two digits; under 7 seconds | No regrouping, ones-only regrouping, tens-only regrouping, two successive regroupings, then borrowing through a zero. Match the relative frequencies to the actual assignment for readiness checks. |

### Two range details to settle when specifying the generator

- **Under 20:** literally means a top number no greater than 19. For the core fact bank, subtracting a single digit is the most direct preparation for column subtraction. If both operands may be 10–19, that introduces some two-digit-minus-two-digit work ahead of tier 4. The rule must explicitly say which interpretation is intended. A boundary problem such as 20 − 7 belongs in tier 3 under a strict “under 20” definition.
- **Up to 100:** 100 is three digits and 100 − 7 requires borrowing through zero. Recommend core tier-3 top numbers 20–99, with 100 treated as an explicit later boundary extension rather than quietly sampling it as an ordinary two-digit item.

For tier 5, a broad general-purpose pool could span top numbers 100–999 and bottom numbers 10–99. Do not assume uniform sampling of that range matches B160: first match its hundreds, result lengths, and borrowing frequencies to the reported assignment. All results remain nonnegative; leading zeros are not required.

### Features worth tracking

| Feature | Example | Why a single borrow flag misses it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No regrouping | 386 − 42 | Establishes execution without a column exchange. |
| Borrow into ones only | 352 − 27 | Ones become 12; the reduced tens still suffice. |
| Borrow into tens only | 324 − 51 | Ones are straightforward; the tens need a hundred. |
| Borrow in both columns | 332 − 57 | The first exchange leaves too few tens; a second is needed. |
| Borrow through zero | 302 − 57 | A hundred must supply the zero tens before supplying the ones. |

For each problem, retain the ordered ones-digit pair and the effective fact after regrouping, such as 12 − 7. Also retain the effective tens operation **after the ones borrow**, the zero pattern, answer length, and exact operand pair. These support both reusable feature statistics and detection of a slow exact fact.

A wrong answer may match several possible errors. Label it a suspected pattern, then compare performance on a related fresh problem; do not claim to know the learner's reasoning from one answer.

## 4. Adaptation and advancement

### Favor weak material without creating a repetitive loop

A starting allocation for ordinary practice could be:

- 50% slow or inaccurate material at the current tier;
- 30% varied current-tier material, including under-sampled features;
- 20% due review from earlier tiers.

These percentages are initial engineering choices, not findings from a study. Adjust them after observing actual learning and engagement. Give every eligible feature a nonzero sampling opportunity. An unseen feature is unknown, not mastered. Weight actual mathematical errors more strongly than correct-but-slow responses, and keep recognition failures separate.

Lower tiers can track exact facts because their pool is small. Higher tiers should combine exact-item history with pooled feature history and sample uncertainty. Do not create thousands of sparsely observed combinations and pretend each has a reliable mastery estimate.

After a mathematical error, give compact feedback and allow correction on the same problem. Then revisit the underlying fact or feature after intervening problems and on a later day. Immediate retries are useful teaching but are not independent evidence of mastery. Repeat facts deliberately after a gap; a permanent no-duplicates rule would defeat this remediation goal.

Persist the random seed and selection state for reproducibility. “Seeding toward weaknesses” means weighted selection conditioned on learner history; a random seed alone cannot provide adaptation.

### Preserve the eight-answer idea, but distinguish its evidence

The exact requested behavior is: advance when the last eight independent first attempts are correct and each meets the current tier's target. A genuine slow or incorrect first attempt breaks that sequence; retries never repair it. Recognition failures or interrupted attempts provide no timing evidence and should not be replaced with fabricated fast times.

Eight successes are encouraging, but there is no identified research basis for treating eight as sufficient for the whole tier. As a simple illustration, a learner with an 80% chance of a fast-correct answer has a 0.8^8 = 16.8% chance of passing one prespecified eight-question sequence. Repeatedly looking for a streak creates further opportunities to pass. This calculation assumes independent, equally difficult attempts; real adaptive questions violate those assumptions and can cluster around a narrow skill.

**Recommended refinement for review:** retain eight fast-correct answers as the visible milestone, but collect them on a representative readiness check after current-tier feature coverage has been established. Keep its generator separate from weakness-weighted practice. Earlier-tier warm-ups must not qualify a learner for the next tier.

Possible initial check composition:

- Tiers 1–2: eight distinct facts, varied digit combinations, with limited trivial cases and meaningful crossing-ten coverage in tier 2.
- Tier 3: predominantly two-digit-minus-one-digit items, including both borrowing and no borrowing, plus a small under-20 review component.
- Tier 4: a balanced mix of borrowing and no borrowing, including a zero-ending top number and varied result lengths.
- Tier 5: all five regrouping categories shown above, plus three varied items drawn to match the assignment mix.

These quotas are proposals. They improve coverage but do not transform an eight-item check into a validated test. A short fresh review on a later day provides useful retention evidence; a single later miss should prompt focused review rather than erase an earned tier.

Keep accuracy and speed separate in reporting. A correct slow answer deserves positive feedback and further fluency practice. Repeated errors call for a worked example or brief parent assistance, not pressure to answer faster.

## 5. Draw-only input and trustworthy timing

### One writable workspace, separate interpretation of the answer

Present the stacked calculation with generous space above and between digits. Put one continuous ink surface over the full question area. Beneath the subtraction line, use a stable, right-aligned answer band whose capacity depends on the tier, never on the expected answer's length.

- Strokes anywhere in the problem area remain visible.
- Recognize only answer-band ink. Printed operands, the subtraction line, and borrow annotations must never enter the digit recognizer.
- Keep scratch and answer strokes distinguishable internally, including undo/erase operations.
- Preserve ink through resizing, pointer cancellation, and resumable state where appropriate.
- Use large native buttons for undo, clear, confirmation, and navigation. No typing, tracing, spoken answers, or multiple-choice answer fallback under this brief.

### A multi-digit submission hazard

A learner may write the ones digit first, pause, and then write the tens or hundreds. An idle timeout cannot reliably tell whether a one-digit response is finished or the learner is midway through a longer answer. Nor may the UI decide completeness by inspecting the expected answer.

Recommendation: begin with a large **Check** action. The learner still draws the whole answer; Check commits it. Measure the gate to the final answer stroke associated with that submission, and record the tap and recognition overhead separately. Later removal of this action should require demonstrated reliability on actual handwriting, especially right-to-left entry. A tap-based commitment is not an alternative answer-entry mode.

### Measure three different things

| Measure | Definition | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Written response time | Fully visible prompt to final answer-stroke completion for the committed first response | Tier speed gates; includes thinking, writing, and borrow marks before completion. |
| Input components | First scratch ink, first answer ink, final ink, commit, and recognition timestamps | Diagnose interaction overhead; first ink is not a pure measure of thinking time. |
| End-to-end assignment time | Start to completion, including actual transitions and corrections under the assignment's rules | Compare with the reported 10-minute target. |

Never start the clock only when writing begins: that excludes the reasoning being trained. Never include recognizer processing in the child's arithmetic speed. Never subtract an assumed fixed “motor time”; the actual amount varies with answer length, strokes, corrections, and device.

Warm the local recognizer before timed practice. Low-confidence or disputed recognition should offer confirmation or redraw without recording a math error; exclude those attempts from fluency evidence if the original completion time cannot be trusted. Include an “I wrote something else” path for confident misreads too. Do not use the correct arithmetic result to steer recognition toward that result.

Backgrounding, pausing, hints, and reloads should make a round ineligible for a clean speed-gate observation. Preserve the learning record and continue supportively. The child never loses the ability to finish a problem after the target time.

## 7. How to establish that it helps

### Before practice

Record a few ordinary comparable assignments' time, first-pass accuracy, corrections, and use of marks. Separately try a brief balanced sample from each relevant tier to locate slow facts versus procedural difficulty. Since the learner already works at B160, foundational checks should be brief; do not require weeks of easy work merely because a new app starts at tier 1.

Use the same device and writing tool when comparing app timings. A short digit-copy sample can expose recognizer or writing friction, but should remain a diagnostic rather than something subtracted from every score.

### During practice

Begin with a short supplemental dose, approximately 3–5 minutes, and stop comfortably rather than pursuing an endless streak. Include one focus, mixed review, and an easy finish. Keep the clock unobtrusive; make correct work, progress, and earned advancement visible. Avoid countdown failure, public rankings, or praise that equates speed with intelligence.

### After one to two weeks

Compare fresh, representative performance with the baseline:

- First-pass accuracy and total completion time on the actual assignments.
- Mean response time and slower-tail times on balanced app checks, not just the fastest streak or the median of correct answers.
- Results by borrowing pattern and effective digit fact.
- Recognition-correction frequency and device/input differences.
- Performance retained on the next day and on unseen combinations.
- Willingness to practice and signs that the extra workload is becoming counterproductive.

App improvement alone is not proof of worksheet transfer. Use the reported 90-question/10-minute assignment target as the ultimate outcome, subject to its actual count and correction rules. This small personal evaluation can guide useful changes, but cannot establish a causal research claim.

## Implementation readiness

The evidence supports proceeding with the focused trainer, with these distinctions preserved:

1. **Confirmed:** five requested tier targets, vertical-only prompts, draw-only answers, full writable area, adaptive attention to weaknesses.
2. **Recommended:** representative promotion checks, spaced retention review, a neutral Check action, recognition-safe timing, short sessions, and a separate worksheet-readiness measure.
3. **Still to specify:** exact tier-2 operand bounds, treatment of 100 in tier 3, tier-5 distribution matching the assignment, and whether the additional readiness safeguards should gate promotion or appear only in parent reporting.
